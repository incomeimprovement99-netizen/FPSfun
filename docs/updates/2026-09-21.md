The owner's list, in one message: a lobby that is one place instead of two
tabs, the battle royale's own settings in it, no map thrown over the screen on
the way out of the dropship, a better dropship, clothes and masks on the

## Shipped

**Milestone 144, one place to start a match.** The Play tab was fourteen
buttons and nothing else. Every choice those buttons obeyed lived on a tab
called 1v1, in three boxes that had nothing to do with each other. Playing a
battle royale with a friend meant opening the 1v1 tab, changing a box that said
"Arena: 1v1 / 1v1v1" to the battle royale, scrolling past two boxes about
something else to the one that was not, and only then making the match. The
owner put it plainly: "having to pick the 1v1 tab and then change to BR to play
with a friend is kind of bad."

Now the modes are a list down one side, and the one you pick opens its own
options beside it. Only its own: the arena asks which of the five maps, how
many bots and how good they are; the battle royale asks the squad size, the
rules, the bot squads, the ring's pace and what you land with; the range asks
about its dummies; the two courses ask nothing, because they are one button
each. One green button starts it, and the button beside it makes the same match
with friends and copies the invite link, translating the mode across on the way
-- which is the piece of work the player used to have to do in their head.

Two things came with it:

- **The ring has a pace**: slow, normal or fast, multiplying every wait and
  close while the circles and the damage stay where they are. A fast match is a
  shorter match, not a harder one, which matters because the radii are what the
  map was built around. It travels in the host's welcome packet; a guest running
  its own clock would be standing outside a ring nobody else can see.
- **The dropship ride is the ride.** Boarding threw the full map up over
  everything, so the first thing anyone ever saw of a match was a map, with the
  ship, the sky and the island behind it. The minimap and the ship's own line
  say where it is going now, and M opens the big one. A redeploy straight into
  the sky keeps its two-second glance, because that one is useful.

Nothing moved but the controls themselves: every box kept its id, so what reads
them is unchanged, and a new check holds the page and the table of modes
together, so a mode can never quietly stop offering the options it obeys.

## Found while working

**No figure in this game has ever had a muzzle.** A figure's gun looked for a
marker called "muzzleflash", which is the name the *first-person* view model
gives its own flash. Figures clone the display model, a separate build the view
model never touches, so the marker was never there and the code quietly gave
up and returned nothing. Every bot and every friend has therefore fired without
a muzzle flash, and every one of their tracers was drawn from the middle of
their chest instead of the end of the barrel. That is the owner's "the bullet
tracers don't always start from the end of the barrel", and it was worse than
reported: for everyone but you, they never did.

The test that should have caught it counted the frames a flash was *meant* to
be lit rather than whether there was a sprite to light, so it passed while
nothing was drawn.

**Milestone 145, how a figure holds a gun, and what it is wearing.** The muzzle above, and three more:

- **Stocks through chests.** The animation library is a pistol library: it has an aim pose with two hands out
  in front and nothing at all for a rifle, so a long gun is hung off the chest at a point we choose and both
  arms are reached onto it. That point was five centimetres *inboard* of the shoulder, and a stock is behind
  the grip, so on every rifle the stock ended up beside the neck. It hangs in the shoulder pocket now,
  outboard of the joint where the torso is not, and a gun too long for that is pushed forward until it
  clears: 22 cm for an SMG, 27 for the longest gun in the game.
- **The floating hand.** The support hand reached for the handguard whether or not the arm was long enough,
  and on the LMGs it was 65 cm away from a 55 cm arm, so it stopped short and hung there. It slides back
  along the gun until it has hold of it now, which is what a person does: the handguard on an SMG, a quarter
  of the way back on a rifle, half on an LMG.
- Every number about the hold is in `src/config/figure.json` rather than in the middle of the rigging.

**Milestone 146, the operators get dressed.** Ten pieces of kit, a different set each: a plate carrier with
pouches, a daypack, a helmet with a rear lip and rails, shades on a strap, a gas mask with a snout, a filter
can and lenses, a hood that stands off the back of the head, a cap peak, shoulder plates, thigh pouches and
knee pads. Built from boxes and cylinders in the operator's own colours rather than downloaded: a prop can be
a scan, but a garment has to be rigged and weighted to the body that wears it, while a plate carrier is
strapped to a chest and can hang off the chest bone and be right. Each piece is welded into one mesh per
material, so five pieces on twelve figures is not six hundred things to draw.

**Milestone 147, the dropship as a shape.** It was fifteen boxes. It is a lofted hull now: seven
cross-sections with the skin stretched over them, 168 triangles, a lit bay with benches you ride in, four
nacelles on two swept wings, twin canted fins, and navigation lights red to port and green to starboard.

**Milestone 148, the spray, measured against what the range draws of it.** The wall drew the *aimed* pattern
whether or not you were aiming, so hip-fired your rounds could never follow it: 69 cm out at 20 m, measured.
And the recoil spring only ran between frames, not between the shots of a frame, so at 25 fps four or five
shots stacked on each other with no recovery and the pattern a burst drew depended on the machine. Both
fixed, and the line is a path with a band round it now rather than a row of dots to land on: a dozen
magazines averaged, with how far they wandered from one another drawn as a ring at each point.

**Milestone 149, what a player on a real ping actually experiences.** We interpolate and we reconcile, and
both were checked in isolation; the thing they are *for* had never been measured. The test transport takes a
fixed one-way delay now, and a match played at 60 and at 120 ms round trip says what a player on the other
side of a country or an ocean gets: six rounds into the figure you can see land on the player it stands for,
the figure is behind rather than ahead of where they may be (a figure drawn ahead would be a hit on somebody
who was never there), and the buffer it is drawn behind is the gap between states rather than more.

**Milestone 150, the arenas stop being boxes.** Seven of the modes are played in the arenas and every piece
of cover in them was a grey box. A CC0 prop stands in for the box now: its mesh is not drawn, and a stack of
crates, a run of barriers or a rack is drawn in the same space. The collider never moves, and the check
proves a map has the same collision before and after it is dressed, which is the whole safety of it.

That is also why only about half the boxes are dressed. A crate two thirds the height of the box it replaced
would be cover you could see over but not shoot over, and one narrower than its box would be a bullet
stopping in mid air, so a prop may only take a box it fills: one that comes in sizes (a crate, a barrier, a
rack) is stretched to the box exactly, within the proportions a thing of that kind plausibly has, and one
that does not (a drum) has to fit as it is. Where nothing fits, the box stays a box.

## The movement: what is in it, what changed, and what is next

The owner asked to keep working on it and to say plainly what could be better and whether we made it better
yet. So:

**What is in, and measured.** Ground acceleration in three bands, air strafing, the slide (with its boost,
its deadslide rule and its roll), the mantle and its superglide window, the climb, the wallbounce, the
bunny hop, the coyote window, jump fatigue, the zipline, the JOLT dash, and the speed and jump paint. All of
it is checked against the Apex wiki and the reference weapon files frame by frame by `tools/movesim.ts`, and
the numbers live in `src/config/movement.json` in the units the engine stores them.

**What changed today.** A **double jump** (44 hu, one until you touch ground, a wall or a rope) and a **wall
run** (along a wall you are already moving along, a bit over half gravity for 1.2 s, and a kick that throws
you off it and up). Both are **off** unless the lobby's Movement box asks for them. That is deliberate and
worth saying: everything else in the movement is Apex's, measured, and these two are ours, so they are kept
apart rather than folded in. The owner can turn them on per match and tell us whether they stay.

**What could still be better, in the order I would do it.**

1. ~~**The camera between the moves.**~~ **Done the same day (Milestone 153).** A slide leaned and a landing
   rolled, and between them the view sat flat: a jump out of a slide dropped the lean the moment it left the
   ground and picked it up again when it came down, so the middle of every chain was the one part that did
   not move with the body. The air leans into the way you are steering now, 2.4 degrees at full, in faster
   than it goes out and smaller than the slide's, because in the air you steer rather than carve. Measured
   in a real page: a scripted jump and strafe leans to 0.68 of full at the diagonal and returns to nothing
   on the ground.
2. ~~**Momentum through a mantle.**~~ **Done (Milestone 154).** It spent every bit of it: you reached the
   ledge standing still and built the speed again. 55% of what you arrived with now comes out on top along
   the way you were facing, capped at a sprint so a slide into a ledge does not throw you off the far side.
   Measured: 221 hu/s on the ledge against a sprint's 260, where it used to be nothing.
3. ~~**The wall run's ending.**~~ **Done (Milestone 154).** It keeps a clock as a backstop, but what ends it
   now is the wall: steer off it and it lets go, and it has to be the same wall it started on. Measured in a
   page: on the wall for the whole run, gone the frame after you steer away.
4. ~~**A slide that steers.**~~ **Done (Milestone 155).** It held its line because holding a direction only
   added acceleration along itself, which over the length of a slide is a drift and not a turn. It turns the
   speed you already have now, up to 55 degrees a second, and the wish pushes along your line of travel
   only: both at once turned a slide at twice the rate the cap says, which made the cap a number that meant
   nothing. Measured: 16 degrees over 0.3 s against a cap of 16.5, still sliding, speed kept.
5. ~~**Air control off a superglide.**~~ **Withdrawn: I was wrong.** The air move is the Source-family
   accelerate (add toward the wish, up to a 60 hu projected cap, at 500 hu/s²), which is what Apex,
   Titanfall and every game in that family do, and it is what makes air strafing work at all. Loosening it
   would not make a superglide feel better; it would make every jump in the game float. What is true is that
   the glide's exit is exact and its *feel* was the camera's, which is item 1, and that is done.

All five movement items from this morning's list are now closed: four built and measured, one withdrawn
because it was wrong. The next movement work is not on this list; it wants the owner to play the two extra
moves and say whether they stay.

## A wardrobe, and a body I had never measured

**Milestone 159.** The owner asked for operator gear: outfits, full masks, shades, goggles. Milestone 157
built the wardrobe and put one set on each of the five operators. What was missing was the part a player
touches, so this adds it: ten outfits instead of six, three builds, a picker on the Loadouts tab, and the
choice carried across the wire so a friend sees what you put on.

The wire is worth a line. The choice rides **beside** the operator id as one short string,
`lk` = "urban|heavy|wrap,goggles", rather than inside the id. That is on purpose. If I had packed it into
the id, a page running an older build would have looked up "vanguard|urban|heavy" in its operator table,
found nothing, and drawn the first operator in the list: everybody turns into Vanguard the moment one
player is on a stale tab. Beside it, the older build ignores a field it has never heard of and draws the
right operator in its own clothes, which is exactly what it should do. Going back to the operator's own set
is said in the clear mask rather than by leaving the field out, or the other side would keep the old
clothes forever, and the codec check now covers both.

Then the part I did not go looking for. I took a snapshot of one outfit in all three builds to check the
picker, and the lean and regular figures had **bare arms and legs** where the heavy one was properly
dressed. The fit table in `outfits.json` said the upper arm was 62 mm thick. I had written that number
myself, by eye, in Milestone 157. The arm is 84 mm at the shoulder. Every sleeve in the game was sitting
inside the arm it was supposed to cover, and the body was coming through it in stripes; the heavy build was
only covered because its cloth is 45% thicker, which was hiding the defect rather than not having it.

So I measured instead of guessing. `tools/checks/body.ts` reads the mannequin's own .glb, takes every
vertex of the skinned mesh, puts it in the frame of the bone that owns it (the joint with the most weight)
and measures how far it sits from that bone's axis. That is the body's radius there. Six samples along each
bone, each one the widest the body gets in the stretch it governs, and the numbers are in the config with
the check standing over them: if the model is ever swapped, the check fails until the profile is measured
again.

And because a limb is not a cylinder, the garment stopped being one. A piece is now turned along that
profile rather than extruded at one radius, so a sleeve is 84 mm across the shoulder and 61 mm at the
elbow, and a trouser leg swells at the calf muscle and narrows at the ankle. The check that matters is the
one an eye would otherwise have to make: no garment in the wardrobe touches the body at the leanest build,
with the clearance printed in millimetres for each.

**And then the cards caught me out.** With the picker on the screen I read the ten cards as a player would.
HOODIE said "a hood up over a plain top"; there was no hood. GHILLIE said "ragged strips over everything";
it was a plain jacket in green. TRACKSUIT said "two stripes"; there were none. Eight of the ten outfits
were the same six garments in a different colour, and three of them were describing clothes that did not
exist. That is worse than a missing feature: it is the game telling a player something untrue in their own
language.

So three more pieces went in, and they are the ones that change an outline rather than a colour: a **hood**
standing up off the back of the head with a brow and a collar (HOODIE and GHILLIE), **ragged strips** hung
in a spiral off the body and the thighs, laid out by count rather than at random so the same outfit is the
same outfit on every screen (GHILLIE), and a **stripe** down the outside of each arm and leg (TRACKSUIT).
Five different sets of garments across the ten outfits now, where there were two.

The check that came with them is the one I would keep if I could keep only one: **what the card says is what
the figure wears.** It reads each outfit's own description, and if the words say hood, rags, stripes,
shorts, boots or sleeves, the outfit has to be wearing the piece that means it. A blurb rewritten to promise
something new fails until the something exists.

**What could still be better.** The torso is still one box tuned by eye, and the spine bone leans back, so
the tops of the shoulders stand outside the jacket: that is the pale patch at the collar in `outfit-close`.
The limbs got a measured profile today and the body should get one too, a centre and a half-depth per band
up the bone. `tools/checks/body.ts` already prints the numbers that fix would need, and it is ranked second
in `docs/ASSET_GAP.md`. Under it: a second head shape, and a sleeveless carrier, which are the two silhouette
moves left that clothes on this rig can still make.

## The body, measured up the spine instead of across it

**Milestone 160.** The wardrobe's last visible defect, and the one I had written into the ranked list an
hour earlier rather than fixed: a pale patch at the top of the shoulders, on every figure, in every outfit.

The limbs got a measured profile this morning. The body did not: it was one box, three numbers, typed in by
eye. The reason that could not work is the same reason the sleeves were wrong, one dimension harder. **The
spine bone leans back.** A vertex 45 cm up the chest lands 18 cm behind that bone's own axis, and a garment
built on the bone leans with it. So a single box centred on an average of the body is wrong in three ways
at once, and now I can say by how much: 360 mm wide at a waist that measures 249, 220 mm deep at a chest
that measures 300, and its middle 90 mm behind where the collar's middle actually is. A jacket that is
80 mm too shallow is a chest standing out of the front and back of it.

The body is six bands now, each with its width, its depth and where its middle sits front to back, measured
off the model by the same tool. The middle runs from +20 mm at the hips to **-69 mm** at the collar. Eighty
nine millimetres of lean, written down in the config instead of guessed at twice.

The jacket follows them: a tube of rectangular sections up the bone, seven rings of four corners, capped at
the hem and the collar so it is not open at either end. The ghillie's strips hang off the same bands, so
they sit on the body rather than around an average of it. The check is the limbs' check again: the garment
has to clear the body at every band, on the leanest build. Worst case, 24.8 mm.

**One thing I had wrong and checked before writing it down.** I had assumed the kit in `gear.ts` (the
plates, the vest, the pack) hung off the same two numbers and would have to move with them. It does not:
the kit has its own numbers in `gear.json` and hangs off the upper spine bone, which is why it never showed
the same fault. So `fit.torso` and `fit.torsoAt` are simply gone. What is worth doing next is measuring the
kit against these bands the way the clothes now are, because a plate carrier 170 mm deep on a chest that
measures 300 is a number nobody has checked either.

## The movement, at the frame rate a player actually has

**Milestone 161.** The owner keeps saying the same thing about movement, and it is the right thing to keep
saying: it has to be smooth, it is the core mechanic. Today's movement work closed the five items on this
morning's list. So I went looking for what the list did not know to ask, and found it in the test harness
rather than in the game.

**Every number in `tools/movesim.ts` is measured at 144 frames a second.** That is this machine. A player
on a 60 Hz laptop integrates the same movement in frames four times longer, and nobody had ever asked
whether they get the same result. If they do not, they are playing a different game: a jump that carries
further, a sprint that arrives sooner, a slide that runs on. It is the most expensive kind of jank, because
the player who has it cannot see it. They have never seen the other one.

So the simulator runs at any frame rate now, and six scripted runs go through it at 30, 60 and 144. Five of
them came back clean: a sprint, a walk from standing, a jump, an air strafe and a turn into a run all end
within a couple of centimetres and **0.0 hu/s** of each other. The ground acceleration was already
integrated through its speed bands inside the frame (that was a fix from an earlier day, and this is the
first time anything has confirmed it holds at 30 fps), and gravity is trapezoidal, which is exact.

**The slide was wrong.** At 30 frames a second it ended 10.6 cm further along and **2.1 hu/s faster** than
the same slide at 144. The cause is in the shape of its own friction: a slide's decay depends on its speed,
`dv/dt = -(a + b(v - c))`, which is an exponential, and taking the rate off once a frame is Euler's method
on an exponential. Euler's method is wrong by an amount that depends on how long the frame was.

`slideDecay()` solves it in closed form instead: the exponential while the speed is above the knee, the
straight line the friction becomes below it, and the crossing between the two solved rather than stepped
over. The spread fell to 3.4 cm and 0.5 hu/s, and every measured slide timing in the file still passes,
which is the point: the closed form is what the small steps were converging to all along.

**What could still be better.** The last few centimetres are the position step, not the physics: position
advances by `velocity x dt` once a frame, so at 30 fps each step is a 23 cm-long guess at where the speed
was during it. Making it trapezoidal, or substepping the whole controller at a fixed 120 Hz, would take it
to nearly nothing - and would move every measured number in the file for under a centimetre of gain. I have
left it, named it in the check, and set the tolerances to say which is which: speed to 1 hu/s because speed
compounds, position to 4 cm because a player cannot feel 4 cm after two seconds. If the owner ever plays at
30 fps and something feels off in a slide, that is the note to come back to.

## The ability numbers belong to the match, not to the page

**Milestone 162.** The owner, on the same message as the 240: "ensure that the number of dashes is
configurable and stuff like that for each of the abilities that we can choose from when we are creating a
1v1 for example (or ffa with a buddy and bots)".

He is right about the *where*, and that is the part worth saying out loud. The dash already had four boxes
in Settings, and Settings is the wrong place for them: what a dash is worth is a property of the game being
played, the way the gun class and the rounds to win already are. Two friends agreeing six dashes and a short
recharge should be able to do it where they pick the mode, and it should be the same for both of them
without either one touching a menu.

So there are nineteen numbers now, three or four per kit, and they sit in the lobby: RUNNER's dashes, how
far one goes, how long it takes and how often one comes back; MEDIC's patch, its time, its cooldown;
SCOUT's pulse range, how long it shows them, its cooldown; HOOK's grapple range, pull speed, cooldown;
SMOKE's cloud radius, how long it lasts, its cooldown; WARD's wall width, its life, its cooldown. The dash
goes to six charges now, up from a cap of four, because six is what was asked for and the HUD's pips thin
to fit.

**The host's numbers are the match's.** They ride in the welcome next to the gun class and the rounds, and
only what the host changed is sent, so most matches carry nothing at all and an older build plays its own.
The bots read the same objects the player does, so a match with six dashes has bots with six dashes. And
they go away with the match: leave, and you have your own back.

That last one caught my own test out, which is worth writing down. I first checked it with a tuned host and
a "plain" guest, and the guest came out of the match still holding the host's six dashes. The code was
right; the check was wrong. Two puppeteer tabs are one browser and share one localStorage, so the "plain"
guest had read the host's numbers as its own at load. Now each page is given its own and they differ: the
host's six dashes of twelve metres, the guest's three of eight. In the match the guest has six; out of it,
three. That is the thing I actually wanted to know.

**The Settings boxes are still there** and write the same store, because they are the same four numbers.
Two places that disagreed would be worse than either.
