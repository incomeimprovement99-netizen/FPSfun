# 2026-09-21

The owner's list, in one message: a lobby that is one place instead of two
tabs, the battle royale's own settings in it, no map thrown over the screen on
the way out of the dropship, a better dropship, clothes and masks on the
characters, more free assets, and four named visual bugs. This is the first
day of working through it.

## Shipped

**Milestone 144, one place to start a match.** The Play tab was fourteen
buttons and nothing else. Every choice those buttons obeyed lived on a tab
called 1v1, in three boxes that had nothing to do with each other. Playing a
battle royale with a friend meant opening the 1v1 tab, changing a box that said
"Arena: 1v1 / 1v1v1" to the battle royale, scrolling past two boxes about
something else to the one that was not, and only then making the match. The
owner put it plainly: "having to pick the 1v1 tab and then change to BR to play
with a friend is kind of bad."

Now the modes are a list down one side, and the one you pick opens its own
options beside it. Only its own: the arena asks which of the five maps, how
many bots and how good they are; the battle royale asks the squad size, the
rules, the bot squads, the ring's pace and what you land with; the range asks
about its dummies; the two courses ask nothing, because they are one button
each. One green button starts it, and the button beside it makes the same match
with friends and copies the invite link, translating the mode across on the way
-- which is the piece of work the player used to have to do in their head.

Two things came with it:

- **The ring has a pace**: slow, normal or fast, multiplying every wait and
  close while the circles and the damage stay where they are. A fast match is a
  shorter match, not a harder one, which matters because the radii are what the
  map was built around. It travels in the host's welcome packet; a guest running
  its own clock would be standing outside a ring nobody else can see.
- **The dropship ride is the ride.** Boarding threw the full map up over
  everything, so the first thing anyone ever saw of a match was a map, with the
  ship, the sky and the island behind it. The minimap and the ship's own line
  say where it is going now, and M opens the big one. A redeploy straight into
  the sky keeps its two-second glance, because that one is useful.

Nothing moved but the controls themselves: every box kept its id, so what reads
them is unchanged, and a new check holds the page and the table of modes
together, so a mode can never quietly stop offering the options it obeys.

## Found while working

**No figure in this game has ever had a muzzle.** A figure's gun looked for a
marker called "muzzleflash", which is the name the *first-person* view model
gives its own flash. Figures clone the display model, a separate build the view
model never touches, so the marker was never there and the code quietly gave
up and returned nothing. Every bot and every friend has therefore fired without
a muzzle flash, and every one of their tracers was drawn from the middle of
their chest instead of the end of the barrel. That is the owner's "the bullet
tracers don't always start from the end of the barrel", and it was worse than
reported: for everyone but you, they never did.

The test that should have caught it counted the frames a flash was *meant* to
be lit rather than whether there was a sprite to light, so it passed while
nothing was drawn.

**Milestone 145, how a figure holds a gun, and what it is wearing.** The muzzle above, and three more:

- **Stocks through chests.** The animation library is a pistol library: it has an aim pose with two hands out
  in front and nothing at all for a rifle, so a long gun is hung off the chest at a point we choose and both
  arms are reached onto it. That point was five centimetres *inboard* of the shoulder, and a stock is behind
  the grip, so on every rifle the stock ended up beside the neck. It hangs in the shoulder pocket now,
  outboard of the joint where the torso is not, and a gun too long for that is pushed forward until it
  clears: 22 cm for an SMG, 27 for the longest gun in the game.
- **The floating hand.** The support hand reached for the handguard whether or not the arm was long enough,
  and on the LMGs it was 65 cm away from a 55 cm arm, so it stopped short and hung there. It slides back
  along the gun until it has hold of it now, which is what a person does: the handguard on an SMG, a quarter
  of the way back on a rifle, half on an LMG.
- Every number about the hold is in `src/config/figure.json` rather than in the middle of the rigging.

**Milestone 146, the operators get dressed.** Ten pieces of kit, a different set each: a plate carrier with
pouches, a daypack, a helmet with a rear lip and rails, shades on a strap, a gas mask with a snout, a filter
can and lenses, a hood that stands off the back of the head, a cap peak, shoulder plates, thigh pouches and
knee pads. Built from boxes and cylinders in the operator's own colours rather than downloaded: a prop can be
a scan, but a garment has to be rigged and weighted to the body that wears it, while a plate carrier is
strapped to a chest and can hang off the chest bone and be right. Each piece is welded into one mesh per
material, so five pieces on twelve figures is not six hundred things to draw.

**Milestone 147, the dropship as a shape.** It was fifteen boxes. It is a lofted hull now: seven
cross-sections with the skin stretched over them, 168 triangles, a lit bay with benches you ride in, four
nacelles on two swept wings, twin canted fins, and navigation lights red to port and green to starboard.

**Milestone 148, the spray, measured against what the range draws of it.** The wall drew the *aimed* pattern
whether or not you were aiming, so hip-fired your rounds could never follow it: 69 cm out at 20 m, measured.
And the recoil spring only ran between frames, not between the shots of a frame, so at 25 fps four or five
shots stacked on each other with no recovery and the pattern a burst drew depended on the machine. Both
fixed, and the line is a path with a band round it now rather than a row of dots to land on: a dozen
magazines averaged, with how far they wandered from one another drawn as a ring at each point.

**Milestone 149, what a player on a real ping actually experiences.** We interpolate and we reconcile, and
both were checked in isolation; the thing they are *for* had never been measured. The test transport takes a
fixed one-way delay now, and a match played at 60 and at 120 ms round trip says what a player on the other
side of a country or an ocean gets: six rounds into the figure you can see land on the player it stands for,
the figure is behind rather than ahead of where they may be (a figure drawn ahead would be a hit on somebody
who was never there), and the buffer it is drawn behind is the gap between states rather than more.

**Milestone 150, the arenas stop being boxes.** Seven of the modes are played in the arenas and every piece
of cover in them was a grey box. A CC0 prop stands in for the box now: its mesh is not drawn, and a stack of
crates, a run of barriers or a rack is drawn in the same space. The collider never moves, and the check
proves a map has the same collision before and after it is dressed, which is the whole safety of it.

That is also why only about half the boxes are dressed. A crate two thirds the height of the box it replaced
would be cover you could see over but not shoot over, and one narrower than its box would be a bullet
stopping in mid air, so a prop may only take a box it fills: one that comes in sizes (a crate, a barrier, a
rack) is stretched to the box exactly, within the proportions a thing of that kind plausibly has, and one
that does not (a drum) has to fit as it is. Where nothing fits, the box stays a box.
